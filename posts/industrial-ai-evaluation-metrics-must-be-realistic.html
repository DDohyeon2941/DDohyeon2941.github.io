Why AI Evaluation Metrics Must Be Realistic in Manufacturing

2026.05.30 · Manufacturing AI · Evaluation Metrics · KPI Design

Workplace examples have been generalized for public disclosure.

Contents

  1. An unattainable target is not a useful criterion
  2. Evaluation criteria define responsibilities
  3. Evaluate recommendations even when they are not applied
  4. Realistic criteria are harder to define
Manufacturing AI projects often set overly idealized evaluation criteria.

Criteria disconnected from reality are eventually ignored. Model performance means little if people in the actual setting cannot trust it.

Evaluation criteria should not be adjusted only after a model is finished. They determine what it optimizes and need to be realistic from the outset.

An unattainable target is not a useful criterion

One project already met the existing quality standard, but a request was made to achieve an even stricter standard.

A higher target seemed worth pursuing, until we examined the data.

We needed to inspect the target distribution and a simple baseline first. With low-variation targets, it is important to compare a complex model against mean-based predictions.

Tree models also have structural limitations in predicting extremes. Capturing extreme values in a target with small variation was an unrealistic demand.

An unattainable criterion had been added to an already satisfactory situation. It would produce an evaluation number without a useful interpretation.

Evaluation criteria define responsibilities

Another project developed a model recommending process conditions to meet desired target quality. Evaluation used quality measurements for individual targets.

Designing its criteria changed my perspective: evaluation was more than measuring how accurately a model predicts.

It defines what AI is responsible for and where human judgment begins.

This perspective makes it important to distinguish model evaluation from the scope of human judgment.

Model evaluation scope

Evaluation targets must match what the model can reasonably be responsible for. Treating every outcome, including those due to other causes, as model performance obscures that responsibility.

Human judgment scope

When interpreting quality indicators required further investigation, responsibility for reviewing causes remained with the relevant person. The model’s role was to indicate when human intervention was needed, not solve everything.

Both criteria clarified the respective roles of AI and people.

Evaluate recommendations even when they are not applied

AI recommendations are rarely applied 100% of the time in manufacturing, especially during initial adoption. It is unrealistic to expect experienced engineers to follow unfamiliar recommendations immediately.

This led me to design a counterfactual monitoring metric:

“What outcome might have occurred if the recommended conditions had been used?”

Evaluation criteria should follow an understanding of actual use. Whether a recommendation was applied must be distinguished from whether its direction was appropriate. Specific operating criteria and calculation methods are not disclosed.

The design did not reach actual operation. Whether AI was used and whether it made a good recommendation are separate questions.

Without that distinction, nonuse cannot be attributed clearly to model quality or trust.

Realistic criteria are harder to define

Requirements such as every inspection meeting specifications, maximizing attainment of strict quality targets, or fully satisfying process-capability criteria are easy to state and difficult to dispute. They may nevertheless be unsuitable for actual use.

Realistic criteria require understanding the process, agreeing responsibility with industry colleagues, and designing who responds when AI is wrong.

This should happen before model development, because the criteria define what the model optimizes.

Criteria detached from reality can make a well-built model optimize something with little practical meaning.

Evaluation design in manufacturing AI combines understanding the setting, allocating responsibility, and reflecting reality.
