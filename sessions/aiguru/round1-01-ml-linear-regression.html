Collection (01) — the most fundamental step Revised

Deciding which data to collect is already the start of decision-making. If the variables are chosen poorly, every subsequent step can lose its meaning.

Three common problems in data collection:
· You cannot tell whether 0 is a real zero or a collection failure. Without metadata recorded at collection time, you cannot recover that distinction later.
· Different sources may use different alignment rules. If sensor A records every 1 second and sensor B every 5 seconds, the data change depending on which timestamps you use to join them.
· Deciding which data to collect requires domain knowledge. It is a decision that calls for process knowledge more than ML knowledge.

Preprocessing (02) — addressing collection problems

Many problems do arise during preprocessing, but their causes often originate during collection. Preprocessing is largely about dealing with the consequences of collection.

Evaluation (05) — a poor criterion can make you confidently deploy the wrong model

On an imbalanced dataset, predicting the majority class for every case can yield 95% accuracy. If the evaluation criterion is wrong, even your judgment of whether a model is good or bad is wrong.

Key terms — establish shared definitions first
TermPrecise meaningCommon ambiguous wording

01 Linear regression 15 min
A model that describes how y changes as X increases by summarizing their relationship with one straight line.
Linear = the change in y is constant whenever X increases by 1 Revised
What “linear” means precisely

If y increases by 10 when X goes from 1 to 2, and also by 10 when X goes from 50 to 51, the relationship is linear. The change must remain constant across the range of X.

In a nonlinear relationship, the change varies across ranges. If reaction speed increases by 2 as temperature goes from 10 to 20 degrees, but by 15 from 60 to 70 degrees, it is nonlinear.

Coefficients are calculated using the whole dataset, rather than separately for each range. The model does not tell you the range of X over which its coefficients are valid; you must judge this from the data range and residual plots.

When it fits well

When the relationship between X and y is approximately linear, there are few variables, and interpretation matters.

When it fits poorly

Nonlinear relationships (curves or inverted U shapes)
Many outliers — a single outlier can pull the entire regression line toward itself
Strong interactions between variables

Why use linear regression?

Its coefficients have meaning: you can say, “When X increases by 1, y changes by β.” This is useful when interpretation matters.

Cautions when interpreting coefficients
  • ✕
    Coefficient magnitude ≠ variable importance
    Age (0–100) versus annual salary (0–100,000,000): different units produce different coefficient magnitudes. Do not directly compare coefficients for variables measured in different units.
  • ✕
    A low R² can undermine confidence in coefficients
    Even if a coefficient is available, a low R² can make its numerical value difficult to trust.
  • !
    In multiple regression, read coefficients as “holding other variables constant”
    “If X increases by 1, y changes by β” → more precisely, this is conditional on all other variables remaining unchanged.

Sign reversal is one possible consequence of multicollinearity

Multicollinearity means one variable can be explained by others (knowing age nearly lets you predict experience).
Sign reversal is one possible effect of multicollinearity. It does not always occur.

Possible effects of multicollinearity:
· Unstable coefficients — even a small data change can cause large coefficient changes
· Coefficients may reverse sign
· Individual coefficients may be unreliable even with a high R²

Incorrect interpretation versus careful reading

✕ “Older people earn less”
✓ “Among people with the same experience, being 1 year older corresponds to KRW 0.8 million less in annual salary” — but the coefficient itself is difficult to trust here.

Diagnosis: if removing one variable and refitting substantially changes another coefficient, that signals multicollinearity. Check pairs with an absolute correlation |r| > 0.8.

03 Overfitting / generalization 15 min
Training performance (train)
↑ High
Memorizes the training data
Test performance (test)
↓ Low
Does not fit unseen data
Overfitting means memorizing even the noise in training data. As model complexity increases, training performance improves, but test performance eventually declines.
Overfitting versus process change — distinguish the causes Added

Both appear as “patterns learned on the training data no longer fit new data.” Their causes, however, require very different responses.

Overfitting: the underlying pattern is still valid, but the model memorizes noise → collect more data or simplify the model.
Process change: the underlying pattern has changed → collect data under the new conditions and retrain.

How to distinguish them: “Did the process actually change?” Events such as equipment replacement, material changes, or seasonal changes indicate process change. If nothing changed but performance declines, first investigate overfitting.

Does adding data solve overfitting?
  • ✓
    Partly true
    With more data, noise can average out and the underlying patterns remain.
  • ✕
    Biased collection does not help
    Even 100,000 observations of normal operation cannot support defect prediction if none cover defective conditions.
  • ✕
    Excessive model complexity remains a limitation
    In that case, reducing model complexity is more appropriate than adding data.

04 Conditions for linear regression 15 min
Residual = actual − predicted value. Residuals help you examine not just “how much the model is wrong,” but “how it is wrong”.
1 Linearity — X and y should have a straight-line relationship Often violated
Intuition
Age and productivity follow an inverted U, but the model tries to fit a straight line.
→ Overprediction in the middle and underprediction at both ends.
→ A U-shaped pattern appears in residuals
Diagnosis
Visually inspect a scatterplot of X versus y.
Look for U-shaped or curved residual patterns.
Response
Try transforming variables (log, sqrt).
Or switch to a tree-based model — often the most practical option
2 Independent residuals — earlier errors should not influence later errors
Intuition
Time-series sales forecasting: yesterday’s shortage also affects today’s sales.
Errors propagate over time.
→ Confidence interval calculations become incorrect
Diagnosis
Plot residuals in time order.
Waves or trends signal a violation.
Response
Use a time-series model.
(Covered in Week 6)
3 Homoscedasticity — error variance should remain constant across predictions
Intuition
Salary prediction: errors are small in the KRW 20–30 million range.
They are much larger in the KRW 100–500 million range.
→ A single RMSE can hide problems in the higher range
Diagnosis
In the residual plot, check for a funnel shape .
Errors increase as predictions increase.
Response
Apply a log transform to y.
Or fit separate models by range.
4 No multicollinearity — variables should not be strongly dependent on one another Critical for interpretation
Intuition
The model cannot distinguish whether y changed because age increased or because experience increased with it.
→ Unstable allocation of coefficients and possible sign reversal
Diagnosis
A residual plot does not reveal this.
Check variable correlations directly (|r| > 0.8).
Large coefficient changes after removing one variable are a warning sign.
Response
Remove one highly correlated variable.
Or combine two variables into one.
Why examine residual plots? Revised
Random scatter and patterns mean different things

Random scatter = the model has captured the available patterns, leaving unpredictable noise. No further structural pattern is apparent to improve.

A pattern (U shape, funnel, etc.) = structure remains that the model has not captured. It signals room for improvement.

A U-shaped pattern is a signal that “there is still work to do”. It gives you a direction: identify what the model missed and improve it.

Healthy — random scatter
No remaining structural pattern is apparent; the model has captured what it can.
U shape → nonlinearity — room to improve
Structure remains. Consider a variable transform or a tree-based model.
Funnel → heteroscedasticity
Errors are large in some ranges; one RMSE can hide this issue.
Trend → dependent residuals
Errors propagate over time. Consider a time-series model.

05 Multicollinearity 10 min
Definition: input variables (X) are so strongly correlated that one can almost be predicted from the others — for example, age nearly predicts experience. Revised
Why it matters — two perspectives
① Coefficient interpretation breaks down

The model cannot distinguish whether y changed because age increased or because experience increased with it.

The allocation of coefficients becomes unstable, and small changes in data can produce large coefficient changes. Even with the same observations, adding one variable can completely change another coefficient.

② A healthy-looking R² can hide it

Multicollinearity does not necessarily damage predictive accuracy. Even with an R² of 0.9, the coefficients can be misleading.

Residual plots can also look clean. This makes the problem easy to overlook when checking only performance numbers.

When is it especially problematic?

Prediction only → predictive accuracy can remain adequate despite multicollinearity, so the model may still be usable.

Coefficient interpretation → critical. If you need to explain “which variable affects y” or “how much y changes when X increases by 1,” the coefficients can become unreliable.

Practical example: “How does the defect rate change if process temperature rises by 1 degree?” If temperature and pressure move together, their individual coefficients cannot be interpreted separately.

Diagnosis — VIF (Variance Inflation Factor)
What is VIF?

VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) quantifies the degree of multicollinearity.
Principle: regress this variable on all the others and examine R². A high R² means the other variables can explain it well, so its VIF increases.

VIF = 1 / (1 − R²); R² = 0.9 gives VIF = 10, and R² = 0.95 gives VIF = 20.

VIF 1 ~ 5
No concern
Variables sufficiently independent
VIF 5 ~ 10
Caution
Interpret coefficients carefully
VIF ≥ 10
Action recommended
Consider removing or combining variables
Quick diagnosis
r > 0.8
Start with correlations between variables
Responses
  • ✓
    Remove one highly correlated variable
    Between age and experience, retain the variable with the more direct causal relationship and remove the other. Domain judgment is needed.
  • ✓
    Combine two variables into one
    If age and experience represent essentially the same concept, combine them into one measure such as “years worked,” addressing collinearity without losing that information.
  • !
    Large changes in other coefficients after removing a variable → a collinearity warning
    The quickest check before calculating VIF: compare coefficients before and after removing a variable.

Comparing metrics — it depends on the question
MetricQuestionUnitsStrengthsCautions
RMSE How large are the errors on average? Same as y (KRW 10,000, kg, etc.) Sensitive to large errors — useful for detecting outliers Interpretation depends on the scale of y. To judge whether a KRW 5 million error is large, examine the range of y.
R² How much of the variance in y does the model explain? None (0–1, but can be negative) Comparable regardless of scale 0.9 is not always good. The criterion depends on domain and purpose.
MAPE What is the average percentage error? % (a ratio) Intuitive: “8% average error” is easy to explain to clients. Percentages explode when actual values approach 0. Do not use it on data near zero.
sMAPE What is the symmetric percentage error? % (a ratio) Partly mitigates MAPE’s zero problem; treats overprediction and underprediction symmetrically. Less intuitive than MAPE; still problematic when both actual and predicted values are 0.
How MAPE is calculated — an intuitive explanation
MAPE = Mean Absolute Percentage Error

For each data point, calculate error as a percentage of its actual value, then take the average.

Example: actual annual salary KRW 50 million → predicted KRW 46 million → error KRW 4 million → 8%
Actual annual salary KRW 30 million → predicted KRW 28.5 million → error KRW 1.5 million → 5%
→ MAPE = (8% + 5%) / 2 = 6.5%

Interpretation: “On average, this model is off by 6.5% of the actual value.”

Difference from RMSE: RMSE expresses error in absolute units (KRW 10,000), so you need the scale of y to judge it. MAPE expresses percentage error, allowing a direct interpretation regardless of scale.

When not to use MAPE

Actual values (y) at or near 0: division by zero, or percentage errors that rise into the thousands.

Example: actual defect count = 0, prediction = 0.5 → error 0.5 / 0 → undefined
Actual stock = 1 item, prediction = 3 → error 2 / 1 = 200% → mathematically correct, but distorts the metric.

In these cases, start with RMSE or MAE (mean absolute error), and consider sMAPE if needed.

Choosing metrics for different situations
  • ✓
    Explaining results to clients or management → MAPE
    “8% average error” is more intuitive than “RMSE of KRW 4.2 million,” provided y is not near zero.
  • ✓
    Particularly costly large errors → RMSE
    RMSE squares errors, giving larger errors more weight. Appropriate when one large error poses a decisive risk.
  • ✓
    Comparing models and explained variance → R²
    Comparable across scales. Use it alongside RMSE or MAPE, rather than alone.
  • !
    Relying on a single metric is risky
    High R² and low RMSE but unusual MAPE → predictions may be poor in a particular range, especially where y is small.
What to check when R² = 0.92 Revised
  • ✕
    Is there data leakage?
    Future information in training data can inflate R². Values near 0.99 or 1.0 should make leakage one of your first checks.
  • ✕
    Is the gap between training and test R² large?
    Training R² = 0.92 and test R² = 0.45 suggest overfitting. Test R² matters.
  • ✕
    Do residual plots contain patterns?
    A U shape or funnel indicates systematic errors in particular ranges.
  • ✕
    Are the training and application distributions similar?
    A changed process or a different production line can change the distribution.
  • !
    Use metrics such as MAPE alongside R²
    MAPE shows the average percentage error, which clients find more intuitive than R². However, it is also distorted when actual values are near zero.

07 Understanding R² Reference
R² measures improvement over predicting the mean: 0 means equal to the mean baseline, 1 means perfect, and a negative value means worse than the mean baseline Revised
R² is not limited to 0–1 — it can be negative

A negative R² means the model does worse than simply predicting the mean. Do not use this model as it stands.
This is not a calculation error. Possible causes include different train/test distributions, poor variable selection, or omitted scaling.

Closer to R² = 1 is better? — investigate first Revised

If R² is near 0.99 or 1.0, check these three things first.

· Data leakage: the prediction target is directly included in the training data. Performance is naturally high if the model already knows the future.
· Target included as a feature: while predicting y, X contains a variable almost identical to y.
· Mixed training and test sets: training and evaluating on the same data naturally produces high scores.

When a low R² can be acceptable
  • ✓
    Inherently noisy domains
    Human behavior, financial markets, or healthcare — R² = 0.2–0.3 can still be meaningful.
  • ✓
    The goal is predicting order or direction, rather than exact values
    Getting “A will be higher than B” right may be enough.
  • ✓
    Improvement over the previous model is the criterion
    Moving from R² = 0.1 to 0.25 can be a substantial improvement.
The message throughout this session

R², RMSE, MAPE, coefficients — no single number can tell you “good” or “bad” on its own.
Examine the conditions under which that number was obtained and the assumptions behind it.

In practice, trust comes from explaining a model together with its limitations, rather than simply passing on its output.
