01 How do equations determine the coefficients (β)? 15 min
In Session 1, we defined learning as finding the β that minimizes L. Linear regression is one of the few cases where we can calculate this β directly using an equation.
Loss function SSE — The criterion that determines β
L(β) = Σᵢ (yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² = Σᵢ (yᵢ − β₀ − β₁x₁ᵢ)²
L(β)
A function of β
L depends on the value of β. Changing β changes L.
argmin
The minimum
The value of β where L(β) is smallest: the bottom of its curve.
β̂
Estimated coefficient
The β value at the bottom. This is the number we see as the learned coefficient.
L(β) is bowl-shaped — Find the bottom directly with an equation
Why can we find it directly?

L(β) = Σ(yᵢ − β₀ − β₁x₁ᵢ)² is quadratic in β. A quadratic graph is bowl-shaped (a parabola), with one minimum.

Mathematically, the bottom is where the slope equals 0. Differentiate L with respect to β and solve for a zero derivative to obtain β̂ directly, in one calculation without iteration.
This is the normal equation, explaining why repeated gradient descent steps, as in deep learning, are unnecessary here.

The equation for β₁ in simple regression — Covariance divided by variance
Simple regression solution β₁ = How much X and y move together ÷ How much X varies
β₁ = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²
Numerator
Covariance (how X and y move together)
Is y also large when X is large? Moving together increases the numerator → β₁ increases.
Denominator
Variance of X (the spread of X)
If the X values are very similar, the denominator shrinks → Estimating β₁ becomes unstable.
Practical implications of this equation

If the variance of X (the denominator) is too small, meaning that most X values are similar, even small amounts of noise can greatly change β₁.
Practical example: if temperature in process data stays between 80 and 82 degrees, its effect (β) is hard to trust. The small denominator makes the estimate unstable.

Multiple regression extends this to matrices — The structure stays the same
Simple regression (one variable)

β₁ = Cov(X, y) / Var(X)
A scalar (number) divided by a scalar.

Multiple regression (several variables)

β̂ = (XᵗX)⁻¹Xᵗy
The matrix version untangles the relationships between X variables (XᵗX) and distributes their relationship with y.

When (XᵗX)⁻¹ cannot be solved — Multicollinearity in equations

(XᵗX)⁻¹ is the inverse matrix. If one variable is an almost perfect linear combination of others, for example when age and experience move together, XᵗX approaches a singular matrix and becomes almost impossible to invert.

An unstable inverse makes the calculation β̂ = (XᵗX)⁻¹Xᵗy unstable. The unstable coefficients discussed in Round 1 originate here. Section 03 examines this in detail.

— Pause — More variables and higher degree are different things 8 min
The equation ŷ = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ uses two variables. But even with just one variable, as in ŷ = β₀ + β₁x + β₂x², you can square the same x and include it again . What increases here is not the number of variables but the degree (power)— and these affect the model in entirely different ways.
SVG Increasing degree — How does the curve change with the same x?
Why does “more complex” mean “more curved”?
A line holds one piece of information, while a curve holds different information at different positions

A first-degree line is described by one number, the slope β₁. Wherever x is, the same rule repeats: increasing x by 1 increases y by β₁. Thus, whatever the data looks like, a line can represent only one pattern, its slope. This is what makes it simple.

With a curve (degree 2 or higher), the slope changes with position. It may increase slowly at small x and more rapidly at large x. Several different slopes coexist within one curve. Curvature means that one function can express more patterns. Greater flexibility lets it fit more complex shapes, including bends, peaks, and valleys, in the data. This is why complexity and curvature are connected.

In practice, increasing degree is like creating a new variable

Treat x² in ŷ = β₀ + β₁x + β₂x² as a new column (variable), and the equation has exactly the same structure as multiple regression, ŷ = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂. β̂ = (XᵗX)⁻¹Xᵗy still applies: we have simply added an x² column to X.

This is polynomial regression. Higher degree lets the curve fit data more flexibly, but too high a degree leads to overfitting that follows the noise. This connects directly to Week 6, “Equations behind model failure.”

02 R² = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot — What do the numerator and denominator measure? 15 min
In Round 1, we understood R² intuitively as how much better a model is than predicting the mean. Now we trace that intuition through the equation.
Coefficient of determination R² — The explained proportion
R² = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot
SS_res = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²
What the model cannot explain
The sum of squared residuals between actual values and the model predictions. Smaller means a better fit.
SS_tot = Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)²
Total variation (baseline)
The sum of squared differences between actual values and predictions using only the mean (ȳ). The baseline without a model.
Visualizing SS_res vs SS_tot
SS_tot (baseline)
100%
SS_res (residuals)
20%
Here SS_res is 20% of SS_tot → R² = 1 − 0.2 = 0.8. The model reduces 80% of the error from predicting the mean.
What exactly does “smaller SS_res means a better fit” mean?
SS_res = Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² measures “how close the points are to the regression line or the model's prediction curve” numerically. The shorter the vertical distance (residual) between each point and the prediction line, the smaller the sum of those squared distances, SS_res.
SVG Small SS_res — Points lie close to the line
The precise meaning of “the model fits well”

A good fit means that model predictions ŷᵢ are close to actual yᵢ throughout the data. Fitting one or two points while badly missing the others does not qualify: those few large residuals are squared and inflate SS_res.

Conversely, small residuals across all points make total SS_res small, suggesting that this model's predictions are reliable.

Why can R² be negative? — Read the equation
If SS_res > SS_tot, then R² < 0

If SS_res / SS_tot exceeds 1, R² = 1 − (a number greater than 1) is negative.

This means the model's sum of squared residuals (SS_res) exceeds that of simply predicting the mean (SS_tot). The model is worse than the mean. This is the mathematical basis for our Round 1 warning against using a model with negative R².

Why question R² near 0.99? — An unusually small numerator
What does SS_res ≈ 0 mean?

For R² to approach 1, SS_res must approach 0. That means almost every yᵢ was predicted accurately.

In real data, ε (noise and unmeasured variables) is rarely zero. If SS_res ≈ 0, the model may have memorized even ε (overfitting), or future information may have leaked into X. This is the mathematical basis for questioning R² very close to 1.0 in Round 1.

Multicollinearity and R² — Why can R² look fine while coefficients fail?
R² examines only ŷ; coefficients examine each β separately

R² measures the predictive power of the whole equation, ŷ = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂. It does not reveal how that power is divided between β₁ and β₂.

With multicollinearity, only the allocation between β₁ and β₂ may be unstable; ŷ itself, their combined result, can remain relatively stable. Thus R² may look fine at 0.9 while individual β values are unreliable, as we saw in Round 1.

Discussion question
“Knowing that SS_tot is the baseline error from predicting the mean, why might R² = 0.3 be acceptable for one dataset but poor for another?”
What does R² mean when using a tree model?
R² is not specific to linear regression. In R² = 1 − SS_res/SS_tot, we need only two values, yᵢ (actual) and ŷᵢ (predicted). The algorithm that generated ŷ does not appear in the equation , so it applies unchanged to trees, random forests, and any model solving a regression problem.
SVG R² is independent of the form of f — A line or steps
What does R² tell us about a tree?

A tree's ŷ is a staircase built from means within regions. It has no regression coefficients β or regression line, but still produces a prediction ŷᵢ for each observation.

Compare ŷᵢ with actual yᵢ to obtain SS_res, then compare it with SS_tot from predicting only the mean to measure “how much better this tree predicts than the mean” using exactly the same yardstick as linear regression. R² does not assess the model's structure (line, steps, or curve); it is a common measure for comparing models with different structures.

However, the interpretation differs — Do not read it as a regression coefficient

A high R² in linear regression can suggest a strong linear relationship explained by β, but that interpretation does not carry over to a tree.

A tree's R² only says that its ŷ is closer to yᵢ than the mean is. To understand which variables matter and why, examine feature importance separately, as we will in Week 03. R² is a common measure of predictive power, while feature importance is an explanation specific to that model — They serve different purposes.

Discussion question
“If linear regression and a tree model both have R² = 0.7, why can you compare them on the same basis? What aspects cannot be compared that way?”
— t-test and F-test — What does “significant” mean mathematically? 15 min
While R² measures how much is explained, t-tests and F-tests ask “whether we can distinguish it from chance”.
The two tests ask about different things: a t-test examines one variable; an F-test examines the whole model.
t-test
“Is this one variable (β₁) significant?”
Unit of inquiry: one coefficient
F-test
“Is the whole model significant?”
Unit of inquiry: the whole regression equation
Visualizing the t-test — “Does this one variable matter?”
Both tests follow the same logic: assume “β = 0 (no effect)” and examine how unlikely the observed value is under that assumption. If it is sufficiently extreme, in the distribution's tail, reject the no-effect assumption. This is what significance means.
SVG t-test — Distribution of t under β=0
Test statistic t = Coefficient ÷ Standard error
t = β̂₁ / SE(β̂₁)
β̂₁
Estimated coefficient
Estimated from the data. The numerator measures its distance from 0.
SE(β̂₁)
Standard error — Variability of the estimate
How much β̂₁ would vary if we resampled the data. A smaller denominator (a more stable estimate) gives a larger t.
How to read it — What does a large t mean?

t = “How many standard errors β̂₁ is away from 0.”

Even a large β̂₁ can produce a small t if SE(β̂₁) is also large (high variability). Conversely, a small β̂₁ can produce a large t if SE(β̂₁) is very small (a stable estimate). The key is not coefficient magnitude, but how far the coefficient is from 0 relative to its variability.

After t: “What is the probability of a result this extreme by chance?” = p-value
A t or F statistic is only a distance. The probability of obtaining that distance by chance is expressed as a probability (%) by the p-value.
. In other words, the test statistic defines the observed situation (t or F), while the p-value expresses how unusual that situation is.
SVG p-value — The area beyond the observed value
Definition p-value
p = P(|T| ≥ |t_observed| | H₀: β = 0)
H₀: β = 0
Null hypothesis
The assumption that this variable has no effect. Begin by assuming it is true.
|t_observed|
The value defining the observed situation
The t (or F) obtained from actual data, summarizing the observed result in one number.
P(... | H₀)
Conditional probability
The probability of a value at least as extreme as this t, conditional on H₀ being true.
Recap — Observed statistics (t/F) and p-values have different roles

t and F statistics = A numerical description of the observed situation in the data (a distance or ratio).
p-value = A probability of a result that extreme occurring by chance, assuming the null is true.

As t or F moves further from 0 into the distribution's tail, the p-value decreases. A small p-value means that such a result would be rare if the null were true, giving reason to question the null hypothesis of no effect.

“If you receive p-value = 0.5, explain it in one sentence beginning ‘If the null hypothesis is true...’”
Visualizing the F-test — “Does the whole model matter?”
SVG F-test — Distribution of F under all β=0
Test statistic F = Explained variance ÷ Unexplained variance
F = (SS_tot − SS_res) / p  ÷  SS_res / (n − p − 1)
MSR (numerator)
Variance explained by the model
(SS_tot − SS_res) is the same explained quantity used in R². Divide it by the number of variables (p) to obtain average explanatory power per variable.
MSE (denominator)
Unexplained variance
SS_res divided by the remaining degrees of freedom (n−p−1): the average error per residual degree of freedom.
F-tests and R² start with the same explained quantity

The numerator of F, (SS_tot − SS_res), is the model-explained portion in R² = 1 − SS_res/SS_tot.

R² divides this quantity by SS_tot to obtain a proportion, while F compares it with MSE (average residual size) to ask how large the explanatory power is relative to residual error. R² describes magnitude; F tests that magnitude against noise. The same ingredients answer different questions.

Recap — t-test vs F-test
Aspectt-testF-test
What is tested? One coefficient (β₁ only, or β₂ only, ...) The entire regression equation (β₁, β₂, ... simultaneously)
Null hypothesis β₁ = 0 β₁ = β₂ = ... = 0
Equation t = β̂₁ / SE(β̂₁) F = MSR / MSE
Practical implication “Is there a basis for keeping this variable in the model?” “Is there a basis for using this model?”
F may be significant while every individual t is not — The connection to multicollinearity

The whole model (F) may have substantial explanatory power while none of its individual variables (t) are significant.

Multicollinearity increases SE(β̂) because of the instability of (XᵗX)⁻¹ discussed in Section 03. The larger denominator in t = β̂/SE(β̂) reduces t, making individual variables nonsignificant. Yet their combined predictive power, assessed by F, can remain strong. This has the same root as “R² looks fine, but the coefficients cannot be trusted.”

Discussion question
“If an F-test is significant but all t-tests are nonsignificant, what diagnosis should you perform first?”

Expand matrix multiplication element by element — What does XᵗX calculate?
To see why (XᵗX)⁻¹ becomes unstable, first examine exactly what XᵗX calculates in each cell. Below, we expand XᵗX using age and experience data for five people.
SVG XᵗX — Matrix multiplication expanded element by element
Recap — Matrix multiplication (XᵗX) ultimately calculates dot products

Each cell of XᵗX is a dot product of two column vectors (Σ xᵢxⱼ). Diagonals pair a variable with itself (related to variance); off-diagonals pair different variables (how they move together).

Thus the essence of multicollinearity is that “the off-diagonal dot product is too large relative to the diagonal dot products”. For a more intuitive view, examine the vectors' directions in the next subsection.

Why do large off-diagonals cause problems? — Examine the determinant
The equation for the inverse (XᵗX)⁻¹ includes the determinant (ad−b²) in the denominator. As b (the off-diagonal dot product) increases, b² increases → ad−b² approaches 0 → The entire inverse grows rapidly. Drag the slider right to see this in real time.
2×2 inverse matrix equation When the denominator ad−b² approaches 0
(XᵗX)⁻¹ = 1 / (ad − b²) × [d, −b / −b, a]
a, d (diagonals)
Each variable's own variance
How widely a variable varies within the data. Larger values increase the denominator and stabilize the inverse.
b (off-diagonal)
The variables' dot product — Their overlap
As b approaches √(ad), ad−b²→0. When the denominator becomes 0, all cells of the inverse diverge toward ∞.

Why do signs reverse? — A direct result of denominator collapse

When the determinant is very small, cells (1,1) and (2,2) of the inverse contain 1/det × d, a very large positive value, while cell (1,2) contains 1/det × (−b), a very large negative value. Multiplying these extreme values by Xᵗy in β̂ = (XᵗX)⁻¹Xᵗy allows even small data changes to flip β₁ from positive to negative or vice versa.

The reversal of the age coefficient in Round 1 results from this denominator collapse: the age–experience dot product (b) is too large relative to their variances (a, d), collapsing the determinant.

In equations — Instability of (XᵗX)⁻¹
Multiple regression solution β̂ = (XᵗX)⁻¹Xᵗy
β̂ = (XᵗX)−1 Xᵗy
XᵗX
Matrix of relationships between variables
A matrix describing how pairs of variables move together. It is healthier when variables are more independent.
(XᵗX)⁻¹
Inverse matrix — Allocating each variable's share
The operation that distributes the relationship with y among variables. This allocation becomes unstable when variables are entangled.
What happens when age ≈ experience?

If age and experience move together (r = 0.88), their directions in XᵗX point almost the same way.

Computing the inverse separates those nearly identical directions to allocate each variable's share. When they are almost parallel, this separation is mathematically unstable. Even small data changes can greatly alter the shares for age and experience, and even reverse their signs.

VIF — Measuring this instability numerically
Diagnostic metric VIF (Variance Inflation Factor)
VIF = 1 / (1 − R²ⱼ)
R²ⱼ
R² when regressing this variable on the others
The R² for how well age can be explained by experience and the remaining variables. 0 means complete independence; values near 1 indicate substantial redundancy.
1 − R²ⱼ (denominator)
The information remaining independently
If R²ⱼ→1, the denominator→0. VIF grows rapidly as the denominator approaches 0, which is the source of instability in (XᵗX)⁻¹.

For the age–experience example (r = 0.88), R²ⱼ ≈ 0.77 → VIF ≈ 4.3. VIF above 5–10 is commonly used as a warning for multicollinearity.
Why does parallelism make R²ⱼ approach 1? — Connect it to cosine similarity
The VIF equation shows that R²ⱼ near 1 makes the denominator approach 0. But why does R²ⱼ approach 1? The answer lies in the angle between vectors. In vector language, independent variables mean two vectors at right angles (90°), while nearly identical variables mean two almost parallel vectors (near 0°). As the angle narrows, R²ⱼ moves toward 1.
SVG Vector independence and multicollinearity — Through cosine similarity
Definition Cosine similarity — The angle between variable vectors
cos(θ) = (x₁ · x₂) / (‖x₁‖ ‖x₂‖)
x₁ · x₂ (dot product)
The off-diagonal of XᵗX
How the variables move together. A larger value indicates vectors closer to parallel.
‖x₁‖‖x₂‖ (denominator)
Normalize by each variable's length
Normalize the dot product into an angle measure from −1 to 1. cos²(θ) = R²ⱼ: the quantity that determines the denominator of VIF.
Independence = Orthogonality = Dot product near 0

In vector language, saying that variables are independent means “their dot product is near 0 (orthogonal, cos(θ)≈0)”.

Conversely, multicollinearity describes “a dot product away from 0 (cos(θ) near 1)”. Since cos²(θ) = R²ⱼ, narrowing the angle completes the chain: R²ⱼ→1, denominator (1−R²ⱼ)→0, VIF→∞.

Thus the surge in VIF has one underlying explanation: As the angle between variable vectors narrows, one variable can explain the other almost perfectly. R²ⱼ approaches 1, and the denominator of VIF collapses toward 0.
